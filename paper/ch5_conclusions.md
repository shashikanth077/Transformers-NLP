#* Conclusions

1. The literature review showed that Transformer models are the state of the art for text classification: fine-tuned encoders exceed 90% accuracy on SST-2, compared with 79–83% for classical models. Lithuanian research confirms that neural networks without pre-training do not necessarily outperform classical methods, while pre-trained models (LitLat BERT, multilingual BERT and T5, Lithuanian Llama2) are now becoming available. The gap identified in the literature was the lack of studies that jointly evaluate quality, cost and data efficiency of Transformers under realistic resource limits.
2. The analysis of the architecture showed that the key advantages of the Transformer are self-attention, which connects every pair of words directly, parallel processing of the whole sequence, and the possibility of large-scale self-supervised pre-training followed by inexpensive fine-tuning. Its main limitations are the quadratic cost of attention with respect to sequence length and the large number of parameters.
3. A reproducible methodology was defined and implemented: ten models from four families (classical TF-IDF, recurrent BiLSTM, fine-tuned Transformers and ready-made Transformers) were compared on the same SST-2 splits on a CPU-only laptop, using accuracy, macro F1, training time, latency, model size, linguistic subsets, learning curves, bootstrap confidence intervals and McNemar tests.
4. The experiment showed that a sufficiently large pre-trained Transformer is clearly the most accurate approach. DistilBERT fine-tuned on only 8,000 sentences reached 88.8% accuracy, significantly more than the best classical model trained on all 65,349 sentences (TF-IDF + linear SVM, 82.9%, p < 0.001), and the ready-made in-domain DistilBERT reached 91.1%. Compact Transformers only matched the classical models (BERT-small 83.7%), and BERT-tiny was the weakest model (76.6%). The BiLSTM trained from scratch (82.8%) gave no advantage over the classical models.
5. The improvement has a clear cost: Transformers are 20–2,000 times slower at inference (1.2–107 ms vs 0.05 ms per sentence on a CPU) and 60–1,700 times larger than TF-IDF models, and fine-tuning on a CPU takes from minutes to hours. In return, pre-training makes them far more data-efficient – with 500 labelled sentences BERT-small was 14.1 percentage points more accurate than TF-IDF, and with 4,000 sentences it matched the classical model trained on 16 times more data – and the larger Transformers were much more robust on sentences with negation and contrast (accuracy drop of 2–4 p.p. versus 8–9 p.p. for bag-of-words models).

**Practical recommendations.** (1) First check whether a public model already fine-tuned for the same task and domain exists – it gives the best quality without any training. (2) If not, fine-tune a distilled Transformer such as DistilBERT on a few thousand labelled examples; this is feasible even without a GPU. (3) When no labelled data exist at all, a zero-shot NLI model gives a usable baseline comparable to classical models. (4) TF-IDF models remain the right choice only when inference must be extremely cheap or the hardware is very limited. For Lithuanian texts, the same procedure can be applied with LitLat BERT or XLM-RoBERTa, which is the most natural continuation of this work, together with repeated runs with several random seeds and GPU-based timing.
