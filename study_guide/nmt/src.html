NMT pipeline

Neural machine translation · end to end

From aligned sentences to a BLEU score

Collect aligned sentence pairs → tokenize → train/validation/test split → encoder/decoder model → attention weights → train with teacher forcing → decode → evaluate with BLEU and human examples. One real example runs through every step: an English → Kannada phrasebook for a visitor in Karnataka. All the maths is computed live on this page.

The example: a phrasebook for a visitor in Mysuru

Someone arriving in Karnataka needs a few dozen everyday sentences: “Where is the bus stand?”, “I will go to Mysuru tomorrow.”, “How much is the coffee?” We hand-built 70 such English–Kannada pairs from 9 patterns (a real, if tiny, parallel corpus) and use them in two ways:

TOY, trained here

A small GRU encoder-decoder with attention (about 16,000 numbers) written for this page. Every number in it can be inspected, and you can retrain it in your browser. Because it learns from only ~48 pairs, it is weak, and that is instructive.

REAL, pretrained

NLLB-200 (Meta AI, 615 million parameters, trained on huge multilingual data), run on the same sentences. It shows what the same pipeline achieves at scale, and its real attention head is used to verify the attention maths.

The pipeline

Honesty noteOur corpus has 70 sentences; real systems use millions (for Indian languages, public collections such as Samanantar contain tens of millions of pairs). The toy model’s modest scores are the expected result of a tiny dataset, not a bug; the pretrained model shows the real-scale picture. Scores on a test set of 11 sentences are very noisy.

Step 1 of 8

Collect aligned sentence pairs

A translation model learns from examples: a sentence in the source language next to its translation. A collection of such pairs is a parallel corpus. “Aligned” means each source sentence is matched with its translation, one to one. The model can only learn what its pairs show, so quality, size and variety of the pairs set the ceiling.
A student learning Kannada from a bilingual phrasebook: each line has the English on the left and the Kannada on the right. If a line is mismatched (the English says “bus stand”, the Kannada says “coffee”), the student learns wrong things and does not know it.
Where pairs come from. Translators’ work (government and news sites published in both languages, subtitles, books), then sentence alignment: matching sentences across the two documents. Classic length-based alignment (Gale and Church) assumes the length of a translation is roughly proportional to the source with noise; modern tools compare multilingual sentence embeddings instead. Then cleaning:
r = (words in Kannada side) / (words in English side)    keep a pair only if rmin ≤ r ≤ rmax
plus removing exact duplicates, pairs in the wrong script, and pairs a trained model finds improbable: the average per-token negative log-likelihood of the Kannada given the English, −(1/T) Σ ln p(yt | y<t, x), is high when the two sides do not match (“dual cross-entropy” style filtering).

Step 2 of 8

Tokenize: turn sentences into numbers

A network needs numbers, so each sentence is cut into tokens and each token is replaced by an integer ID from a fixed vocabulary. Special tokens mark the start (<s>), the end (</s>), padding and unknown words (<unk>). The vocabulary is built from the training data only.
Giving every word in the phrasebook a page number. A word you have never seen has no page: it becomes “unknown”. Kannada attaches endings to words (ಮೈಸೂರು “Mysuru”, ಮೈಸೂರಿಗೆ “to Mysuru”), so a word-level phrasebook needs a separate page for every ending. Cutting words into frequent pieces (subwords) avoids this.

Try it: tokenize a pair

Subwords: byte-pair encoding (BPE), live

BPE starts with single characters and repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair into a new symbol:
(a, b) = argmax(a,b) count(a followed by b)    vocabulary size = (number of distinct characters) + (number of merges)
Frequent words become single tokens; rare words are spelled with a few pieces; nothing is ever unknown. The model NLLB uses a similar method (SentencePiece) with a vocabulary of about 256,000 pieces.

BPE here is learned on the Kannada words of the training pairs. Our translation model itself uses whole-word tokens so every step stays readable; real systems use subwords.

Step 3 of 8

Split: train, validation and test

The pairs are divided into three groups that never overlap. The model learns from the training set. The validation set is used while building the system, to tune settings and decide when to stop. The test set is touched once at the end for an honest score, because a model naturally does well on sentences it has memorised.
A language class: practice sheets (training), a mock exam to decide how long to keep revising (validation), and the real exam with questions the student has never seen (test). Letting exam questions leak into the practice sheets would make the marks meaningless.
Random split with a fixed seed (so results can be reproduced): for N pairs, test ≈ 15%, validation ≈ 15%, training = the rest.
Ntrain = N − Nval − Ntest    no pair appears in two sets: |train ∩ val| = |train ∩ test| = |val ∩ test| = 0
With only 70 pairs a different seed changes which sentences are tested, so scores jump around: a small test set gives a noisy estimate (the standard error of an accuracy-like score is about √(p(1−p)/n); for n = 11 that is up to ±15 points).
Why the “coverage” option?With a corpus this small, a randomly chosen test sentence often contains a word that never occurs in training. Then the model cannot possibly produce it (it only knows words from the training vocabulary), and the test measures vocabulary luck instead of translation skill. Real systems avoid this with subwords and far more data; here we keep the test fair by requiring that every word has been seen. Untick it to see the unknown-word rate jump.

Step 4 of 8

The encoder-decoder model

Two networks work together. The encoder reads the English tokens one by one and produces a vector for every position that summarises the sentence read so far. The decoder then writes the Kannada one token at a time, each time looking at the encoder’s vectors (attention, next step) and at what it has already written. At the end of each decoder step a softmax gives a probability for every Kannada token.
Equations. Es, Et are embedding tables (one row of d numbers per token). GRU is the gated recurrent unit from the RNN/LSTM/GRU guide.
hi = GRU( Es[xi], hi−1 )    h0 = 0,   i = 1 … S    (encoder)
s0 = hS,   st = GRU( Et[yt−1], st−1 )    (decoder; y0 = <s>)
at = tanh( Wc [ ct ; st ] + bc ),   pt = softmax( Wo at + bo )    (ct comes from attention, step 5)

Step 5 of 8

Calculate the attention weights

When the decoder is about to write a word, it should look mostly at the source words that matter for that word. Attention does this in three small calculations: score every source position against the decoder’s current state, turn the scores into weights that sum to 1, and take the weighted average of the encoder vectors. That average is the context the decoder uses.
A translator with the English sentence on the desk. Before writing “ಬಸ್ ನಿಲ್ದಾಣ” she glances mostly at “bus stand”; before writing “ಎಲ್ಲಿದೆ” she glances at “Where”. The glance is a set of weights across the English words.
et,i = ( st · hi ) / √H     (score: how well position i matches the decoder state)
αt,i = exp( et,i ) / Σj exp( et,j )    (weights, sum to 1)
ct = Σi αt,i hi    (context vector)
The dot product is large when two vectors point the same way; dividing by √H keeps scores from growing with the vector length. Transformers use exactly this “scaled dot-product” with learned projections: Q = st·WQ, K = hi·WK, V = hi·WV, and weights = softmax(Q KT / √dk).

Step 6 of 8

Train with teacher forcing

To learn, the model is shown a source sentence and the correct translation. The decoder is fed the correct previous words (shifted by one, with a start token in front) and at every position must predict the next correct word. Giving the right answer as the hint is teacher forcing. The loss measures how much probability the model gave to the right word; gradients flow back through the softmax, attention, decoder and encoder, and the optimiser nudges all weights.
L = −(1/T) Σt=1..T ln pt( yt )    decoder input: ( <s>, y1, …, yT−1 )    targets: ( y1, …, yT = </s> )
Why teacher forcing: all positions are computed in one pass, and a wrong guess at position 3 does not corrupt position 4 during learning. The price is exposure bias: at test time the model must feed on its own earlier outputs, which it never practised. Backpropagation here uses the chain rule exactly as in the earlier guides; one extra piece is the gradient of the attention softmax: with dαi = dc · hi,   dei = αi ( dαi − Σj αj dαj ). The page’s gradients were checked against numerical derivatives (relative error below 10−4).
w ← w − η · Adam( ∂L/∂w ),   gradient norm clipped to 1,   mini-batches of 4 pairs

Step 7 of 8

Decode: produce a translation

At test time there is no correct answer to feed in. The decoder starts with <s>, predicts the next word, feeds its own prediction back in, and repeats until it predicts </s>. Greedy decoding always takes the single most probable word. Beam search keeps the k best partial translations at every step, so one tempting but poor early choice can be recovered.
score( y1..T ) = Σt ln p( yt | y<t, x )    ranking score = score / Tα    (length normalisation, α = 0 means none)
Each log-probability is negative, so every extra word lowers the score; without normalisation beam search prefers short outputs. Beam width 1 is greedy decoding.

Step 8 of 8

Evaluate: BLEU and human examples

BLEU scores a translation by how many of its word sequences (n-grams) also appear in the human reference, with a penalty for being too short. It is fast and repeatable but blind to meaning: a correct translation worded differently scores low. So BLEU is always read together with other measures (here chrF, which compares character sequences and suits Kannada’s long, ending-rich words) and with human judgement of real examples.
For candidate and reference tokens, with n = 1…4:
pn = Σ min( countcand(g), countref(g) ) / Σ countcand(g)    (clipped n-gram precision)
BP = 1 if c ≥ r, otherwise e1 − r/c    (brevity penalty; c, r = candidate and reference length)
BLEU = 100 · BP · exp( ¼ Σn=1..4 ln pn )
“Clipped” means a candidate word cannot be credited more times than it occurs in the reference. A single missing 4-gram makes p4 = 0 and BLEU 0, so on short sentences we add 1 to the counts for n > 1 (a standard smoothing). chrF = (1+β²) P R / (β² P + R) with β = 2, averaged over character n-grams of length 1–6.

Human evaluation: read the examples

Numbers hide what went wrong. For each test sentence compare the reference, the toy model and NLLB, then give an adequacy score (is the meaning right? 1 to 5) and a fluency score (does it read naturally? 1 to 5). Your scores are averaged below and saved in this browser. If you read Kannada, your judgement is the point of this step; if not, use the automatic word-difference marks.

How professionals do itSeveral translators rate each output on adequacy and fluency (direct assessment) or mark errors by type and severity (the MQM scheme). Agreement between raters is reported with Cohen’s κ = (po − pe) / (1 − pe), where po is the observed agreement and pe the agreement expected by chance. BLEU differences below roughly 1 point are rarely meaningful, and results on 11 sentences are anecdotes: real evaluations use thousands of sentences, several references, and significance tests such as bootstrap resampling.
