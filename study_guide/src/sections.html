The project in 60 seconds

  1. Question. Everyone says Transformers (BERT, GPT, ChatGPT…) are the best NLP tools. But how much better are they than older methods when you only have an ordinary laptop and a few thousand labelled examples, and what do they cost?
  2. Method. We built a fair contest on one task, sentiment analysis (is a movie-review sentence positive or negative?). 10 models from 4 families competed on the same unseen sentences: classical word-counting models, a recurrent network (BiLSTM), Transformers we fine-tuned ourselves, and ready-made Transformers.
  3. Answer. A big enough pre-trained Transformer wins clearly (% with only 8,000 training sentences vs % for the best classical model trained on ), but it is 20–2,000× slower at inference. Pre-training makes it data-efficient, and a ready-made in-domain model needs no training at all (%).

Understanding

How a Transformer works (without the fear)

Five ideas, each with something to try. If you can explain these, you can answer most theory questions.

Idea 1: Words become numbers (tokens)

A computer cannot read words. The text is cut into tokens (pieces of words), each token gets an ID, and each ID is looked up in a table that gives a vector (a list of numbers, for example 768 numbers in DistilBERT). Rare words are split into known pieces, so nothing is ever “unknown”. These are real outputs of the DistilBERT tokenizer:

Why “##”? A piece starting with ## continues the previous piece. “unwatchable” = un + ##watch + ##able. The model can guess the meaning of a new word from its parts, like you can.

Then positional encoding adds the position of each token, because attention alone does not know word order (“dog bites man” vs “man bites dog”).

Idea 2: Self-attention, each word looks at all the others

For each word the model computes how much attention it should pay to every other word, then mixes their information accordingly. In the sentence “the plot was not good”, the word “good” should pay attention to “not”. The recipe is one formula:

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q·Kᵀ / √dk ) · V
  • Q (query): “what am I looking for?”   K (key): “what do I contain?”   V (value): “what do I hand over if you pick me”.
  • Q·Kᵀ = a similarity score for every pair of words. softmax turns scores into percentages that add up to 100%.
  • The output for a word = the weighted average of all the values, using those percentages.

Try it: attention calculator

Imagine the word “good” asking: “which words matter for me?” Drag the similarity scores (higher = more relevant). Watch softmax turn them into attention weights.

Why divide by √dk? With larger vectors the dot products grow (roughly like √dk). Unscaled, softmax becomes extremely peaked (one word gets ~100%, the rest ~0%) and learning almost stops. Untick the box and raise dk to see it happen.

Idea 3: Many heads, many layers (the architecture)

One attention is not enough, so the model runs several heads in parallel (each can look for a different relation: negation, grammar, who-did-what). Each layer adds a feed-forward network, residual shortcuts and layer normalisation; layers are stacked. Click a block:

Idea 4: Why is it better than a recurrent network?

A BiLSTM reads one word at a time and must squeeze everything into a small memory. A Transformer reads all words at once, and every word has a direct line to every other word.

Sentence: “the plot was not good , but the acting was brilliant”

Transformer (parallel)

But there is a price. Attention compares every pair of words, so its cost grows with the square of the sentence length (O(n²·d)). For long documents this is a problem (efficient Transformer variants exist). On our short sentences it is fine, and our measurements show where the real cost is: size and speed (see Results explorer).

Idea 5: Pre-train once, fine-tune cheaply

1 · Pre-training

Done by Google/Hugging Face on huge unlabelled text for days on many GPUs. Task: guess hidden words (masked language modelling: 15% of words hidden). The model learns grammar, facts, style. We did not do this. We downloaded the result.

2 · Fine-tuning

We add a tiny classifier on top of the [CLS] vector and train the whole network on our labelled sentences for 2–3 epochs. This is the cheap part (minutes to hours on a CPU) and what we measured.

3 · Using it

Feed a sentence, get P(positive) and P(negative). Or skip step 2 entirely: ready-made models were already fine-tuned by someone else; zero-shot models are asked with a question instead of training.

Pre-training is like a student who has read millions of pages before our exam. Fine-tuning is a short course on our exam’s topic. That is why a Transformer with 500 labels beats a keyword model with 500 labels by 14 points.

We split the test set by simple keyword rules: sentences with negation (not, no, never, n’t…) and with contrast (but, although, though, yet, however).

Training curves

Dev-set accuracy after each epoch (the epoch with the best dev accuracy is kept). Note the BiLSTM’s dev accuracy (~91%) is much higher than its test accuracy (82.8%). The dev set comes from the training distribution (short phrases), the test set has full sentences.

Is a difference real? McNemar test (any two models)

How it works. Both models classify the same 872 sentences. Count the sentences where only A is right (b) and where only B is right (c). If the models were equally good, b and c would be about equal. The exact binomial test asks: how surprising is this split? A small p-value (< 0.05) means the difference is unlikely to be luck.

Background

Literature review and the Lithuanian context

How the literature was searched

Google Scholar and Web of Science (plus ACL Anthology and arXiv). Core query:

("transformer" OR "BERT" OR "self-attention" OR "pre-trained language model") AND ("natural language processing" OR "text classification" OR "sentiment analysis")

For Lithuania the query was narrowed with “Lithuanian” OR “Baltic languages”. About 40 works were kept; seminal ones were added by citation tracking.

The gap we address
  • Almost all results are reported on GPUs with the full training set.
  • Cost (CPU time, latency, size) is rarely reported next to accuracy.
  • Lithuanian work shows deep nets without pre-training do not beat classical methods; data efficiency of compact pre-trained models is insufficiently studied.
  • Ready-made vs fine-tuned models are rarely compared under one protocol.

Key works

Lithuanian research, in four steps

Why did we not use Lithuanian data? A comparable public Lithuanian benchmark with a fixed test set, the same CPU budget and ready-made models for every family was not available for a fair 10-model contest within the course. The method is language-independent: replace SST-2 with a Lithuanian review set and the English models with LitLat BERT (Ulčar & Robnik-Šikonja, 2021) or XLM-R. Say this openly: it is the natural next step, not a hidden weakness.
