Section 1

What is a Transformer?

A Transformer is a neural network that reads one sequence of tokens and writes another. Text is a natural fit: it is a sequence, order carries meaning, and lengths vary. The classic job is translation, so that is our running example. Pick a sentence and watch a real model do it.

Inside, a Transformer is two stacks of layers plus some plumbing. To avoid confusion, a single layer is called an encoder or a decoder, and a group of them an encoder stack or decoder stack.

Section 2

What does attention do?

Attention is the idea that made the architecture work. While the model processes one word, attention lets it look at the other words and pull in information from the ones that matter for that word. Self-attention does this for every word against every other word in the same sentence.

The classic puzzle: what does “it” refer to?

Change one word at the end and the meaning of “it” flips. To read “it” correctly a model has to connect it to the right noun.

Several attention scores per word (multi-head attention)

A single set of weights cannot capture every kind of relationship in a sentence. So each word gets several attention scores at once, one per head. One head might link a pronoun to a possible noun; another might track a nearby verb or adjective. The results are combined, so the final meaning of “it” can carry a bit of several things.

The table lists, for the word “it”, the heads that put the most attention on the two nouns, taken from the real model. Notice how the numbers hardly change between “big” and “small”: DistilBERT is a small, general model, and a single head does not resolve the puzzle on its own. Several heads, several layers and the feed-forward layers all contribute.

Section 3

Training the Transformer

A Transformer behaves a little differently when it learns than when it is used. For training, every example is a pair: a source sequence and the correct target sequence. For translation that is an English sentence and a human Kannada translation. The goal is to learn to produce the target, using both sequences.

Section 4

Inference: writing the answer one word at a time

When the model is used, there is no target sequence to hand to the decoder. It has to produce one. So it generates in a loop: start with just a start token, predict the next word, append it to the decoder input, predict again, and stop when it predicts an end token. At every step the whole output so far is fed back in, not only the last word. The encoder runs once, because the source never changes.

Section 5

Teacher forcing

In training, the decoder is not run in a loop. It is given the correct target sequence (shifted by one, with a start token in front) and asked to predict the next word at every position at once. Feeding the correct answer as the “hint” is called teacher forcing. Two reasons it is used:

  • Errors do not pile up. If the model guesses the first word wrongly, the second prediction is still based on the correct first word, so one mistake does not spoil the rest of the sentence.
  • Speed. All positions are computed in one parallel pass instead of one pass per word.

What to look forPick the 2nd or 3rd branch. Once the first word differs from the reference, everything after it is built on that different start, and the final sentence can drift or change meaning. Teacher forcing avoids this during training because the next input is always the correct word, even after a wrong guess.

Section 6

What are Transformers used for?

Transformers handle most language tasks: language modelling, text classification, translation, summarisation, question answering, named-entity recognition and speech recognition. The encoder layer is the common building block; each application adds its own head on top, depending on the job.

Section 7

Why Transformers beat RNNs

RNNs and their relatives (LSTMs, GRUs) were the standard for language until Transformers arrived. They worked well, and attention was first added to improve them. But they had two weaknesses:

  1. Long-range dependencies are hard. Information between two far-apart words must survive many steps.
  2. They are sequential. Step t cannot start until step t − 1 has finished, which makes training and inference slow.

Convolutional networks (CNNs) compute in parallel, but only words inside the kernel window interact in one layer, so distant words need many layers. Transformers drop recurrence and rely on attention: all words are processed in parallel and the distance between words does not matter.

An honest nuanceParallel does not mean free. Attention compares every word with every other word, so its work grows with the square of the sentence length. That is fine for sentences and paragraphs, and is why long documents need special tricks. The win over RNNs is the short, direct path between words and the ability to compute all positions at once.
