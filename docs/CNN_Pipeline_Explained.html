CNN, step by step

Convolutional neural network · end to end

How a CNN reads an image and learns

Image → Convolution → ReLU → Pooling → Flatten → Dense → Prediction → Loss → Backpropagation → Weight updates. Each step is explained in plain words, with a real-world picture and the maths. The numbers on this page are not mocked up: a tiny CNN runs live in your browser, so you can watch every number change as it learns.

The real-world problem: reading a PIN code on a parcel

Indian PIN codes have six digits (for example 560 001). Parcel-sorting machines photograph the handwritten digits and a model has to say which digit each picture shows. A CNN is the standard tool for this kind of job. To keep every number visible, our model is a miniature version: it reads 8×8 pixel pictures of the digits 0, 1 and 7. The steps are identical to those in a big network that reads a 224×224 photograph; only the sizes differ.

The pipeline (click a step)

Steps 1 to 7 are the forward pass (picture in, answer out). Steps 8 to 10 are learning: measure the mistake, work out who is to blame, and fix the weights. Learning repeats thousands of times.

How to use this pagePick or draw a digit in step 1. That same picture then flows through every later step, and each step shows the numbers for your picture. Use the training buttons in step 10 and watch earlier steps change.
Honest scale noteOur CNN has only 114 learnable numbers. ResNet-50 has about 25 million. Real systems add more filters, more layers, colour channels, normalisation and other tricks, but the ten steps are the same.

Step 1 of 10

Image: a picture is a grid of numbers

A computer does not see a picture; it sees a table of numbers. Each number says how bright one tiny square (a pixel) is. Our digit is an 8×8 table: 64 numbers, from 0 (blank paper) to 1 (full ink).
The sorting machine’s camera measures the darkness of the ink at 64 spots on a small square of the parcel label. That list of 64 measurements is all the CNN ever gets.
A greyscale image is a matrix X with entries X(i, j). Cameras give integers 0 to 255; we divide by 255 so the values are between 0 and 1, which keeps later arithmetic well behaved.
X(i, j) = raw(i, j) / 255    (so 222 → 0.87)
A colour photo has three such matrices (red, green, blue), so its shape is height × width × 3. Ours has one channel: 8 × 8 × 1.

Step 2 of 10

Convolution: sliding a small pattern detector

A filter (also called a kernel) is a tiny table of weights, here 3×3. Place it on a 3×3 patch of the image, multiply each pair of overlapping numbers, and add everything up. A big result means “this patch looks like my pattern”. Then slide one pixel and repeat. The grid of results is called a feature map.
Imagine a small stencil that only lights up where there is a vertical stroke. You slide it over the digit and mark every place it matches. A “1” lights up a lot; a “0” lights up only on its two sides. A second stencil may look for horizontal bars, and a third for slanted strokes. A CNN learns which stencils are useful; nobody draws them by hand.
For filter f with weights Kf (3×3) and bias bf, the value at output position (i, j) is
cf(i, j) = bf + Σu=0..2 Σv=0..2 Kf(u, v) · X(i+u, j+v)
Nine multiplications and one addition per output cell. The same nine weights are reused at every position (weight sharing). Strictly this is cross-correlation (the kernel is not flipped); deep-learning libraries call it convolution anyway, and so do we.

How big is the output?

Output = ⌊(N + 2P − F) / S⌋ + 1
Why share the weights?A fully connected layer from 64 pixels to a 6×6 map would need 64 × 36 = 2,304 weights per map. A convolution needs only 9 (+1 bias). Fewer numbers to learn, and a stroke is detected wherever it appears in the picture.

Step 3 of 10

ReLU: keep the evidence, drop the rest

After convolution some numbers are negative (“the pattern is clearly absent here”). ReLU (rectified linear unit) replaces every negative number with 0 and leaves positive numbers alone. It is the simplest possible “switch”.
A judge writes down only the evidence for a stroke. Where there is none, the note is blank (0), not a negative pile.
ReLU(x) = max(0, x)     slope: ReLU′(x) = 1 if x > 0, otherwise 0
Why have it at all? Without a non-linear step, stacking convolution after convolution would still be one big linear operation, so extra layers would add nothing. The bend at 0 lets layers combine into more complex patterns. The slope (1 or 0) matters later: during backpropagation it decides whether a signal passes through.

Step 4 of 10

Pooling: shrink, keep the strongest signal

Max pooling cuts the map into 2×2 blocks and keeps only the biggest number of each block. The map becomes 4 times smaller, and the result no longer cares about the exact pixel where a stroke was found, only roughly where.
A postman’s tally: “somewhere in this corner of the label I saw a vertical stroke”. Exactly which of the four squares does not matter for the digit’s identity, and handwriting shifts by a pixel or two anyway.
mf(r, s) = max { af(2r+p, 2s+q) : p, q ∈ {0, 1} }    size: 6×6 → 3×3
There is nothing to learn here (0 parameters). The network remembers which cell won in each block (green outline), because backpropagation will send the learning signal only to that cell.

Step 5 of 10

Flatten: lay the maps out as one list

The next layer works on a single list of numbers, so the three 3×3 pooled maps are written one after another: 3 maps × 9 numbers = a list of 27. No calculation happens, only re-ordering.
Spreading all the judges’ notes on one long table, in a fixed order, so a panel can read them one by one.
xf·9 + r·3 + s = mf(r, s)    shape (3, 3, 3) → (27)
0 parameters. The order is arbitrary but must be the same every time.

Step 6 of 10

Dense layer: weighing the evidence

Now every one of the 27 numbers is connected to each of the 3 outputs (one per digit 0, 1, 7). An output is a weighted sum: evidence that supports the digit has a positive weight, evidence against it a negative weight. The result for each digit is called a score or logit.
Three judges, one per digit. Judge “7” puts big positive weight on “horizontal bar near the top” and negative weight on “closed loop”. Each judge adds up their weighted notes and announces a score.
zk = dk + Σj=0..26 W(k, j) · xj    or in matrix form   z = W x + d
W is a 3 × 27 matrix (81 weights), d a bias vector (3 numbers): 84 learnable numbers. The scores can be any real numbers, negative or positive.

All 81 weights W(k, j) (rows = digits 0, 1, 7; columns = the 27 list entries; hover for values)

Step 7 of 10

Prediction: scores become probabilities

Raw scores are hard to interpret. Softmax converts them into probabilities that are positive and add up to 1. The biggest score gets the biggest probability, and the digit with the highest probability is the model’s answer.
The three judges’ scores are turned into “I am 80% sure it is a 7, 15% a 1, 5% a 0”.
pk = ezk / Σm ezm
The exponential makes every number positive; dividing by the sum makes them add to 1. In practice we subtract the largest score before exponentiating (it does not change the result but avoids overflow).

Step 8 of 10

Loss: one number for “how wrong was that?”

The loss compares the prediction with the truth. If the model gave the right digit a high probability, the loss is small; if it gave it a low probability, the loss is large, and very large if it was confidently wrong. Training means making the average loss small.
Marks deducted by an examiner. A hesitant wrong answer loses a few marks; a confident wrong answer loses many.
L = − Σk yk ln pk = − ln ptrue
y is the “one-hot” true answer (1 for the true digit, 0 for the others), so only the true digit’s probability counts. This is cross-entropy. Perfect (p = 1) gives L = 0; p → 0 gives L → ∞. A model that guesses uniformly among 3 digits has L = ln 3 ≈ 1.10.

Step 9 of 10

Backpropagation: who is to blame?

The loss is a number; the network has 114 weights. Backpropagation finds, for every weight, how much the loss would change if that weight were nudged up a little. That number is the weight’s gradient. It works backwards from the loss, reusing numbers saved during the forward pass, which makes it cheap: about as much work as the forward pass itself.
A parcel went to the wrong city. Trace back: the final judge’s weights gave too much score to the wrong digit; the evidence list had a misleading number; that came from a pooled cell; it came from one winning pixel of one feature map; it was produced by the filter’s weights. At each hop we hand out a share of the blame.
The chain rule. If L depends on w through a chain of steps, the sensitivities multiply:
∂L/∂w = (∂L/∂p) · (∂p/∂z) · (∂z/∂x) · (∂x/∂c) · (∂c/∂w)
Tiny example: if y = 3u and u = 2w, then dy/dw = 3 · 2 = 6. Backprop is this idea applied layer by layer, running from the loss back to the filters.

Proof that the maths is right: the numerical gradient check

A gradient is a slope, so it can be measured directly: nudge one weight by a tiny ε both ways, recompute the loss, and divide the loss change by 2ε. If our backprop formulas are correct, the two numbers agree.

Step 10 of 10

Weight updates: a small step downhill

The gradient says which way increases the loss, so we move every weight a small step the opposite way. The step size is the learning rate. Do this for many pictures, again and again, and the loss falls. That is all “learning” means here.
Standing on a foggy hillside and wanting the valley: feel which way the ground slopes up, then take a small step the other way. Steps too small take forever; too big and you leap across the valley.
w ← w − η · ∂L/∂w    (η = learning rate)
This rule, stochastic gradient descent (SGD), is applied to every kernel weight, bias, dense weight and dense bias. One pass over all training pictures is an epoch.

Try 0.005 (slow) and 1.0 (jumpy, may overshoot).

What the big networks add

  • Many more filters and layers. Early layers find edges, middle layers find textures and parts (a wheel, an eye), late layers find whole objects. Our toy has one convolution layer.
  • Colour channels. A filter becomes 3 × 3 × 3 so it looks at red, green and blue together.
  • Mini-batches, momentum/Adam, batch normalisation, dropout. They make training faster and steadier; the gradient idea is unchanged.
  • Same ten steps. From reading digits to spotting a tumour on a scan, it is image → features → scores → loss → gradient → update.
